Australia's Solar Story

3.7M+Rooftop Solar Installations
~40%NEM Generation from Renewables
82%Renewable Target by 2030
#1Per-Capita Solar, Globally

Australia has become one of the world's great solar success stories. With abundant sunshine, falling technology costs, and a network of government incentives, rooftop solar has gone from a niche technology to a mainstream household appliance in less than two decades. As of 2023, over 3.7 million small-scale solar installations have been registered — one of the highest per-capita rates anywhere in the world.

Why does Australia lead the world in rooftop solar? A combination of exceptional solar resources (especially across QLD, WA, and SA), strong government rebates through the Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme (SRES), falling panel costs, and high electricity prices have made the economics of rooftop solar compelling for homeowners and businesses alike.

How Rooftop Solar Works

1. Solar Panels

Photovoltaic (PV) panels convert sunlight into direct current (DC) electricity. The more sunlight, the more electricity generated. Modern residential panels typically produce 300–450 watts each, with a standard system being 6–10 kW.

2. Inverter

The inverter converts DC electricity from the panels into alternating current (AC) — the type used by your household appliances. Modern inverters also monitor system performance in real time.

3. Your Home & the Grid

Electricity your panels generate goes first to your home. Any surplus is exported to the grid, and your retailer pays you a feed-in tariff (FiT) for each kWh exported — typically 5–10 cents/kWh depending on your state and plan.

4. STCs — Your Rebate

When you install rooftop solar, you're eligible for Small-scale Technology Certificates (STCs). Your installer typically claims these on your behalf, passing the value back to you as an upfront discount on the system cost.

The Big Picture — Australia's Renewable Transition

Australia's National Electricity Market is undergoing the fastest energy transition of any comparable grid in the world. Wind and solar capacity is being added at record rates, and states like South Australia have frequently achieved 100% instantaneous renewable energy generation.

South Australia

A world leader in grid-scale renewables. SA has operated for extended periods with more renewable generation than total demand — exporting surplus to Victoria. Home to the famous Hornsdale Power Reserve (the "big battery").

Queensland

Australia's solar capital by installed capacity. QLD's rooftop solar penetration regularly leads the NEM, and its large solar farms contribute significant daytime generation to the grid.

Victoria

Rapidly expanding wind and solar capacity, with major offshore wind projects planned off Gippsland. Victoria's Star of the South project would be one of the Southern Hemisphere's largest offshore wind farms.

New South Wales

The NEM's largest state by demand. NSW has committed to a Renewable Energy Zone (REZ) strategy in key regions — New England, Central-West Orana, and South West — to build the backbone of its clean energy future.

Real-time data: You can track Australia's live renewable energy generation at OpenNEM — a free, beautifully designed dashboard showing current wind, solar, hydro, and gas generation across every NEM region. The Clean Energy Council publishes annual reports on installed capacity, generation, and industry employment.

Australia's environmental certificate schemes create financial incentives for clean energy generation and emissions reduction. Each certificate type represents a measurable environmental outcome.

LGC

Large-scale Generation Certificate

Created by accredited renewable power stations (wind, solar, hydro) for each MWh of eligible electricity generated above a baseline. Surrendered by liable entities to meet their Renewable Energy Target (RET) obligations.

STC

Small-scale Technology Certificate

Created by small-scale renewable systems (rooftop solar, solar water heaters, heat pumps). STCs give households an upfront rebate that effectively reduces the cost of installing a solar system.

ACCU

Australian Carbon Credit Unit

Issued by the Clean Energy Regulator for projects that store carbon or reduce greenhouse gas emissions (e.g. avoided deforestation, soil carbon, landfill gas capture). Each ACCU represents one tonne of CO₂-equivalent avoided or sequestered.

VEEC

Victorian Energy Efficiency Certificate

Created under Victoria's Victorian Energy Upgrades program when accredited businesses install energy-efficient products or systems. Each certificate represents one tonne of CO₂-equivalent greenhouse gas abated.

How certificates drive clean energy investment: Energy retailers and large electricity consumers have mandatory annual obligations to surrender a set number of certificates. If they fall short, they pay a shortfall charge. This creates structured demand that incentivises investment in renewable generation, rooftop solar, and emissions reduction projects.

The National Electricity Market (NEM) is one of the world's longest interconnected power systems, spanning Queensland, New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia, and Tasmania. AEMO manages dispatch, settlements, and market integrity.

Electricity spot prices are set every 5 minutes through a competitive dispatch process. Prices range from −$1,000/MWh to $16,600/MWh depending on supply and demand conditions.

How AEMO Dispatch Works

Every 5 minutes, AEMO runs a dispatch algorithm that matches electricity supply and demand across the NEM:

  1. Generators submit offers — each generator bids a volume of electricity at a specified price (up to 10 price bands).
  2. AEMO stacks bids cheapest to most expensive — renewables typically bid near zero marginal cost; gas and coal bid higher.
  3. The marginal generator sets the price — the last generator dispatched to meet demand sets the regional spot price for all generators in that region.
  4. Settlement occurs every 30 minutes — the trading interval price is the average of six 5-minute dispatch prices.
Price floor & cap: The market price floor is −$1,000/MWh and the market price cap is $16,600/MWh (set by AEMO annually). Sustained high prices trigger the cumulative price threshold mechanism.

Your First Python Program

Python is one of the most beginner-friendly programming languages — readable, forgiving, and used everywhere from web development to data analysis. Here's everything you need to start.

Python — Hello, World!
# Your first Python program
print("Hello, World!")

# Python reads and runs line by line, top to bottom
# Lines starting with # are comments — Python ignores them
# They're notes for humans reading the code

Variables & Data Types

Variables are containers that store information. Unlike many languages, Python figures out the type automatically.

Python — Variables
# Text (called a "string")
name = "Henrique"
city = "Sydney"

# Whole numbers (integers)
age = 30
year = 2025

# Decimal numbers (floats)
height = 1.80
temperature = 23.5

# True/False (booleans)
is_analyst = True
has_degree  = False

# Print values
print(name)           # → Henrique
print(age + 5)        # → 35
print(f"Hi, {name}!") # → Hi, Henrique!   (f-string: put variables inside {})

Making Decisions — If / Else

Python — Conditions
temperature = 28

if temperature > 30:
    print("Hot day — stay hydrated!")
elif temperature > 20:
    print("Nice weather.")
else:
    print("A bit cool today.")

# Comparison operators:
# >   greater than        <   less than
# >=  greater or equal    <=  less or equal
# ==  equal to            !=  not equal to
Indentation matters in Python. The code inside an if block must be indented (4 spaces or 1 tab). Python uses indentation instead of curly braces to define blocks. Getting this wrong is the most common beginner mistake.

Repeating Things — Loops

Python — For Loop
# Loop through a range of numbers
for i in range(5):
    print(f"Count: {i}")
# Output: Count: 0, Count: 1, Count: 2, Count: 3, Count: 4

# Loop through a list
regions = ["NSW", "QLD", "SA", "TAS", "VIC"]
for region in regions:
    print(f"Processing: {region}")

# Loop with index
fruits = ["apple", "banana", "mango"]
for index, fruit in enumerate(fruits):
    print(f"{index}: {fruit}")
# Output: 0: apple  1: banana  2: mango

Reusable Code — Functions

Python — Functions
# Define a function with def
def greet(name):
    print(f"Hello, {name}!")

# Call the function
greet("Henrique")   # → Hello, Henrique!
greet("Anna")       # → Hello, Anna!

# Function with a return value
def add(a, b):
    return a + b

result = add(10, 20)
print(result)       # → 30

# Function with a default argument
def introduce(name, city="Sydney"):
    print(f"My name is {name} and I live in {city}.")

introduce("Henrique")           # → My name is Henrique and I live in Sydney.
introduce("Carlos", "Brisbane") # → My name is Carlos and I live in Brisbane.

Working with Data

Python — Lists & Dictionaries
# Lists — ordered collections
scores = [85, 92, 78, 95, 88]
print(scores[0])          # → 85  (first item, index starts at 0)
print(len(scores))        # → 5   (how many items)
print(sum(scores) / len(scores))  # → average

scores.append(91)         # add to the end
scores.sort()             # sort in place: [78, 85, 88, 91, 92, 95]

# Dictionaries — key → value pairs
person = {
    "name": "Henrique",
    "age": 30,
    "city": "Sydney"
}
print(person["name"])     # → Henrique
person["job"] = "Analyst" # add a new key

# Loop through a dictionary
for key, value in person.items():
    print(f"{key}: {value}")
Python — Reading a CSV with pandas
import pandas as pd

# Read a CSV file into a DataFrame (like a spreadsheet in Python)
df = pd.read_csv("data.csv")

# Explore the data
print(df.shape)        # (rows, columns)
print(df.columns)      # list of column names
print(df.head())       # first 5 rows
print(df.describe())   # summary statistics

# Filter rows
adults = df[df["age"] > 18]

# Select a column
names = df["name"]

# Calculate average
avg_age = df["age"].mean()
print(f"Average age: {avg_age:.1f}")

What Is SQL?

SQL (Structured Query Language) is the language used to talk to databases. It lets you retrieve, filter, sort, count, and combine data stored in tables. It's one of the most in-demand skills in data, finance, and tech — and the basics are genuinely easy to learn.

SQL — Your first query
-- Get everything from a table
SELECT * FROM customers;

-- Get specific columns
SELECT name, age, city FROM customers;

-- The basic structure is always:
-- SELECT (what columns)
-- FROM   (which table)

Filtering Data — WHERE

SQL — WHERE clause
-- Customers older than 25
SELECT name, age FROM customers WHERE age > 25;

-- Customers in Sydney
SELECT * FROM customers WHERE city = 'Sydney';

-- Multiple conditions
SELECT * FROM customers
WHERE age > 18
  AND city = 'Sydney';

-- Range
SELECT * FROM orders
WHERE amount BETWEEN 100 AND 500;

-- Partial text match
SELECT * FROM customers WHERE name LIKE 'H%';
-- % means "anything" — so this finds names starting with H

Sorting & Limiting Results

SQL — ORDER BY & LIMIT
-- Sort by age, youngest first
SELECT name, age FROM customers ORDER BY age ASC;

-- Sort by age, oldest first
SELECT name, age FROM customers ORDER BY age DESC;

-- Only show the top 5 results
SELECT name, age FROM customers
ORDER BY age DESC
LIMIT 5;

-- Useful for finding the "top N" of anything:
-- Most expensive orders, highest earners, latest records, etc.

Counting & Aggregating — GROUP BY

SQL — Aggregation
-- Count how many customers per city
SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS customer_count
FROM customers
GROUP BY city;

-- Total sales by product
SELECT product_name,
       SUM(quantity)   AS total_sold,
       AVG(price)      AS avg_price,
       MAX(price)      AS highest_price
FROM sales
GROUP BY product_name;

-- Only show groups with more than 10 customers
-- Use HAVING (not WHERE) to filter on aggregated values
SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS customer_count
FROM customers
GROUP BY city
HAVING COUNT(*) > 10
ORDER BY customer_count DESC;
WHERE vs HAVING: WHERE filters individual rows before grouping. HAVING filters groups after aggregation. You can't use COUNT() or SUM() inside a WHERE clause — that's what HAVING is for.

Combining Tables — JOIN

SQL — INNER JOIN
-- customers table has: id, name, city
-- orders table has: id, customer_id, product, amount

-- Join customers with their orders
SELECT customers.name,
       orders.product,
       orders.amount
FROM customers
INNER JOIN orders ON customers.id = orders.customer_id;

-- Only returns rows where BOTH tables have a match
-- Use LEFT JOIN to keep all customers (even if they have no orders):
SELECT customers.name,
       orders.product,
       orders.amount
FROM customers
LEFT JOIN orders ON customers.id = orders.customer_id;
-- Customers with no orders will show NULL for product and amount
